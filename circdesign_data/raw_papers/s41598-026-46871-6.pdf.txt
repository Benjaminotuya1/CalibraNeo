Lipid nanoparticle delivery of 
circle RNA vaccine induces potent 
immune responses
Ruohan Yang, Lin Jia & Jiuwei Cui
Circle RNA (circRNA) is a closed-loop nucleic acid resistant to exonuclease degradation. Their enhanced 
stability and potential for efficient protein expression through circRNA modification render them 
promising novel therapeutic agents for cancer treatment. In this study, we systematically investigated 
the antitumor and anti-inflammatory activities of circRNA vaccines in mice, as well as their effects on 
activating antigen-specific immune responses, through a meta-analysis. Subsequently, we further 
validated the immune-activating effects of circRNA using monocytes and T cells isolated from human 
peripheral blood mononuclear cells. We found that circRNA induces the activation and maturation 
of dendritic cells, as well as the responses of CD4 + and CD8 + T cells. These findings demonstrate the 
potential of circRNA to exert significant effects in therapeutic cancer vaccines.
Keywords circRNA, Lipid nanoparticles, Immunotherapy, Malignant tumors, Antigen-presenting cells
Circle RNA (circRNA) constitutes a distinct RNA subtype featuring a covalently closed single-stranded 
structure1. circRNA exhibit non-coding functions such as transcriptional regulation, splicing regulation, signal 
transduction, and molecular sponge activities. These regulatory roles enable them to promote the onset and 
progression of various diseases, thereby influencing the intrinsic mechanisms of disease2–5.
In recent years, the use of in vitro-synthesized RNA to express neoantigens for disease treatment has become 
a focal point for many researchers6. This approach enables the precise and efficient expression of specific proteins 
within cells and tissues7. Currently, the use of mRNA to encode COVID-19 antigens represents one of the most 
prominent applications of this method. However, mRNA vaccines suffer from poor stability, imposing stringent 
requirements on transport and storage conditions 8. Concurrently, mRNA faces the risk of rapid degradation, 
resulting in short-lived neoantigen expression and necessitating repeated dosing for therapeutic efficacy 9. Our 
prior research has demonstrated that, compared to mRNA, circRNA possesses a unique circular structure that 
confers resistance to RNase R digestion, thereby enhancing its stability and positioning it as a novel vaccine 
candidate10. Studies indicate that circRNA has a half-life approximately 2.5 times longer than mRNA, thereby 
prolonging the duration of protein expression11. As illustrated in Fig. 1, circRNA can mediate ribosomal initiation 
and protein translation via the internal ribosomal entry site (IRES) element, even in the absence of a free 5 ′ 
end, thereby enabling efficient antigen presentation12. Moreover, unlike DNA vaccines, circular RNAs carry no 
risk of host genome recombination13 and do not require endosomal processing and cleavage, which can reduce 
antigen presentation efficiency in peptide vaccines14. Established research demonstrates that circRNAs can elicit 
potent T- and B-cell responses, resulting in complete tumor regression in mouse models of melanoma15. Wei et 
al. confirmed that circRNA vaccines provide robust protection against SARS-CoV-2 variants in both mice and 
rhesus monkeys, exhibiting excellent tolerability with no significant adverse effects16.
circRNA holds immense potential as a next-generation RNA vaccine platform. Its unique advantages of 
efficient synthesis and stable high expression have demonstrated promise in vaccines, immunotherapies, and 
protein replacement therapies, offering innovative and effective treatment prospects for human diseases 17. 
Concurrently, integrating lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) with RNA vaccines significantly enhances their therapeutic 
applications18. LNP efficiently encapsulates RNA, enabling large-scale and cost-effective production.
Therefore, building upon these findings, this study investigates the extent to which LNP-encapsulated 
circRNA induces immune responses in mice, thereby exerting antitumor and anti-inflammatory effects. This 
research provides valuable insights and perspectives for future investigations.

Materials and methods
Search strategy
This systematic review and meta-analysis adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and 
Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines19. Internet searches were conducted across the PubMed, Cochrane Library, 
EMBASE, Springer, and ScienceDirect, and OVID databases to retrieve relevant publications from 2020 to the 
present (October 2025). The following search terms were used to identify articles: ‘circle RNA ’ , ‘circRNA ’ , ’ circular 
RNA ’ , and ‘vaccine’ . (PROSPERO ID: CRD420251242287).
Inclusion criteria
Inclusion criteria for meta-analysis studies: (1) circRNA therapy for tumors or infectious diseases delivered via 
LNP; (2) Detailed documentation of intervention measures, experimental groupings, and dosage administration; 
(3) Inclusion of at least one of the following metrics: tumor volume, mouse body weight, and immunological 
parameters; (4) Full-text articles available.
Exclusion criteria
Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) non-mouse animal models or in vitro studies; (2) Data failing to 
demonstrate tumor changes in mice before and after treatment; (3) Reviews, communications, case reports, 
presentations, letters, expert opinions, and editorials; (4) Relevant data unavailable for extraction; (5) Failure to 
meet inclusion criteria.
Data extraction and quality assessment
The current studies primarily comprised preclinical research using animal models. We analyzed data on whether 
mice with tumors or infectious diseases benefit from treatment with a circRNA vaccine. Extracted content 
included: (a) basic study details, including first author and publication year; (b) study characteristics, including 
tumor volume, weight, inhibition rate, mouse body weight, and adverse reactions; (c) elements relevant to risk 
of bias assessment. Using tools developed by the SYRCLE 20, the authors assessed the methodological quality 
and risk of bias in the included trials across domains: selection, performance, detection, attrition, reporting, and 
other biases.
Plasmid construction
The vector was purchased from MIAOLING Biotech (IRES-Circ-GFP-FLAG). Immunofluorescence detection 
was subsequently performed using an anti-Flag antibody (Sigma-Aldrich, F1804). circRNA was synthesized 
using the HiScribe T7 High Yield RNA Synthesis Kit (NEB E2040S). IVT templates were amplified via PCR 
before RNA synthesis (Q5 Hot Start High Fidelity 2x Master Mix). Each 20 µL IVT reaction utilized 1 µg of 
circRNA PCR template. Reactions proceeded at 37 °C for 2 h. Subsequently, IVT templates were degraded by 
incubating with 2 µL DNase I (NEB M0303S) at 37 °C for 20 min. Digestion was performed using 1 U RNase R 
per µg RNA at 37 °C for 30 min, followed by RNase R inactivation at 70 °C for 10 min. Circularization efficiency 
was assessed via RT-qPCR (forward primer:  T C T G A A C C A C A C A A A T G G T C; reverse primer:  A T G G T G G G G 
T A C C C A T C C G C T).

Cell lines
Healthy human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were commercially purchased from Beijing 
VITONLIVIA (D176368). Cell line origin: human. Gender: male. Source tissue: peripheral blood. The cell lines 
used were not misidentified, and there was no contamination during the experiment.
CD14 + magnetic beads (MicroBeads, Miltenyi, 130-050-201) were added at a ratio of 20 µl per 10⁷ PBMCs 
and incubated at 4 °C in the dark for 15 min. Following incubation, peripheral blood mononuclear cells were 
isolated using LS Columns (MASC, 130-042-401). Cells were cultured in 10% 1640 medium supplemented with 
cytokines IL-4 (50 ng/ml) and GM-CSF (20 ng/ml) to induce dendritic cell differentiation.
For in vitro T-cell activation assays, healthy PBMCs were also commercially purchased from Beijing 
VITONLIVIA(D176368). CD3 + magnetic beads (MicroBeads, Miltenyi, 130-097-043). For every 10⁷ PBMCs, 
add 10 µl CD14+ magnetic beads and incubate at 4 °C in the dark for 15 min. Subsequently, isolate CD3⁺ T cells 
using an LS Column and culture them in 1640 medium, promptly adding activated dendritic cells to maintain 
activity. The culture medium contains 10% foetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco), 2% penicillin, 1% streptomycin, 
and 1% levofloxacin, and cells are grown in a humidified environment at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. All cell lines are 
monitored for mycoplasma infection using the Impact III PCR method.
Flow cytometry analysis
The following antibodies target surface markers: Human CD4 (FITC, BioLegend, 300505, 1:100); Human CD8 
(APCy7, BioLegend, 300908, 1:100); human IFN-γ (BD, PE, 344117,1:100; human CD80 (APC, BioLegend, 
305219, 1:100); human CD86 (FITC, BioLegend, 344704, 1:100).
Immunofluorescence
In immunofluorescence experiments, transfected dendritic cells were centrifuged and smeared onto microscope 
slides. Sections were incubated overnight with monoclonal anti-FLAG M2 mouse antibody (Sigma, F1804, 1:500 
dilution), followed by incubation with Alexa Fluor 647-labelled secondary antibody. DAPI was used for nuclear 
counterstaining. Images were acquired using a laser scanning confocal microscope equipped with NIS-Elements 
Viewer imaging software.
RT-qpcr
RT-qPCR analysis was performed in triplicate using Brilliant II SYBR Green qRT-PCR Master Mix (Agilent, 
600825) and a LightCycler 480 (Roche). The relative RNA level was calculated by the ddCt method compared to 
the B-Actin control.

Preparation and purification of in vitro transcribed circRNA
To eliminate double-stranded RNA (dsRNA) contaminants, the crude transcription reaction was directly treated 
with RNase III at 37 °C for 30 min to specifically degrade dsRNA. The reaction was then purified using a silica-
membrane-based RNA cleanup column to remove proteins, salts, and unincorporated nucleotides. And the 
absence of dsRNA contamination was verified using a dsRNA-specific fluorometric quantification kit and ELISA 
with a four-parameter logistic (4PL) standard curve. (Y esaen, 36717ES96).
ELISpot
As mentioned above, ELISpot T cells were collected from peripheral blood and then restimulated with dendritic 
cells (DCs) transfected with circleRNA for 24  h. IFN-γ ELISpot assays were performed according to the 
manufacturer’s instructions (Dakewe, 2110005, Human) to detect IFN-γ-secreting T cells, and the colored spots 
were counted.
Statistical analysis
All data were analyzed using Review Manager 5.4 software. Forest plots displayed pooled risk ratios with their 
respective 95% confidence intervals. Statistical heterogeneity was assessed using the inconsistency index (I 2). 
Data exhibiting substantial heterogeneity were analyzed using random-effects models, whilst those with minimal 
heterogeneity were evaluated using fixed-effects models.
Prism software version 9.2.0 was used to perform statistical analyses. One-way and two-way ANOV A tests 
and an unpaired two-sided Student’s t test were used to compare more than two groups, and differences between 
groups with P-values below 0.05 were considered significant.
Results
Systematic screening
The study screening process is illustrated in Fig. 2. A total of 427 articles met the inclusion criteria. Among these, 
duplicate and irrelevant articles (e.g., studies with inconsistent subjects) were excluded following an abstract 
review. Subsequently, articles with inconsistent methodologies (e.g., solely in vitro cellular experiments or the 
absence of tumor-related endpoints) were excluded after full-text review. Ultimately, ten tumors 15,21–29 and 
eleven infectious16,30–39 disease model preclinical studies were selected for meta-analysis.

Basic demographic characteristics of included studies
Data extracted from the included studies are presented in Table  1. All selected studies were randomized 
controlled trials (RCTs) utilizing mice as experimental subjects. Overall, the animal strains employed were 
predominantly BLAB/C or C57BL/6, with administration primarily via intramuscular or subcutaneous injection. 
Notably, within infectious disease mouse models, three instances involved intranasal administration, and one 
case employed tracheal instillation. Among animal tumor models, ovalbumin served as the neoantigen in four 
instances, representing its most prevalent application.
Risk of bias assessment
A total of 10 studies reported the baseline characteristics of experimental mice, with no significant bias identified. 
The summary of preliminary risk assessment results for selected articles is presented in Table 2.
Body weight changes in all mice before and after treatment
As shown in Fig.  3, we compared the body weight of mice before and after treatment according to different 
administration routes. Subgroup analyses were performed for subcutaneous injection, intramuscular injection, 
intranasal injection, intravenous injection, and other administration routes (including intraperitoneal injection, 
intramuscular injection, and intratracheal instillation). Compared with the control group, there was no 
statistically significant difference in body weight in the vaccine group across different administration routes 
(overall standardized mean difference (SMD) = -0.05, 95% CI = -0.42 to 0.32, P = 0.80). Subgroup analyses 
showed that the pooled effect sizes of different administration routes did not reach statistical significance, and 
the overall between-study heterogeneity was low (I2 = 0%).
Changes in T-cell proportion before and after treatment in all mice
The results demonstrated that the vaccine group exhibited a significantly increased proportion of activated T cells 
compared with the control group (overall SMD = 4.14, 95% CI = 2.67 to 5.61, P < 0.00001). Subgroup analyses 
indicated that the vaccine conferred significantly superior effects relative to the control via subcutaneous, 
intramuscular, and intravenous administration (all P < 0.05), whereas no statistically significant effect was 
observed in the subgroup of other administration routes (Fig. 4).
Assessment of tumor-related parameters in mice
Subsequently, we conducted separate efficacy evaluations on mice within the tumor model, including 
measurements of tumor volume changes. We also documented alterations in immune cells playing pivotal roles 
in tumor treatment, such as macrophages and dendritic cells (DCs).

Immunocellular changes
As shown in Fig. 6, compared with the control group, intervention in the vaccine group significantly increased 
the proportion of macrophages overall SMD = 4.88 (95% CI: 1.65 to 8.11, P = 0.003), with high between-study 
heterogeneity (I2 = 79%, P = 0.0008). Subgroup analyses stratified by administration route revealed a statistically 
significant effect in the subcutaneous injection subgroup (SMD = 5.35, 95% CI: 1.63 to 9.08, P = 0.005) with 
moderate-to-high heterogeneity (I 2 = 63%). The effect in the intravenous injection subgroup did not reach 
statistical significance (SMD = 4.37, 95% CI: -3.65 to 12.38, P = 0.29) and showed extremely high heterogeneity 
(I2 = 89%). The other administration routes subgroup exhibited borderline statistical significance (SMD = 6.23, 
95% CI: 0.00 to 12.45, P = 0.05). The effect in the intramuscular injection subgroup could not be estimated due 
to missing data.
Regarding dendritic cell expression levels, the vaccine group significantly elevated dendritic cell levels

Changes in serum antibody titres
As illustrated in Fig. 8, serum antibody titres in mice with infectious diseases showed a marked increase following 
circular RNA treatment. Compared with the control group, intervention in the experimental group significantly 
increased serum antibody titres (overall SMD = 5.85, 95% CI: 3.69 to 8.02, P < 0.0001), with substantial between-
study heterogeneity (I2 = 79%, P < 0.0001). Subgroup analyses stratified by administration route demonstrated 
significant effects across all routes: subcutaneous injection (SMD = 5.93, 95% CI: 2.82 to 9.04, P = 0.0002); 
intramuscular injection (SMD = 6.08, 95% CI: 1.86 to 10.29, P = 0.005); intranasal injection (SMD = 8.10, 95% 
CI: 5.10 to 11.09, P < 0.00001); and other administration routes (SMD = 2.86, 95% CI: 0.24 to 5.79, P = 0.03). 
Significant differences in effect size were detected among subgroups ( P = 0.004, I² = 63.2%), suggesting that 
administration route serves as an important source of between-study heterogeneity.
As shown in Fig.  9, intervention in the vaccine group significantly reduced pulmonary viral load overall 
(overall SMD = -9.50, 95% CI: -14.70 to -4.30, P = 0.0003), accompanied by high between-study heterogeneity (I2

Serum cytokine changes
We also examined serum cytokine alterations. As shown in Fig.  10, the vaccine group exhibited significantly 
elevated IFN-γ levels overall SMD = 3.11, 95% CI: 1.54 to 4.69, P = 0.0001), with high between-study heterogeneity 
(I2 = 80%, P < 0.0001). Subgroup analyses stratified by administration route demonstrated significant effects 
across all routes: subcutaneous injection (SMD = 1.69, 95% CI: 0.29 to 3.09, P = 0.02); intramuscular injection 
(SMD = 2.59, 95% CI: 1.12 to 4.06, P = 0.0005); intranasal injection (SMD = 8.40, 95% CI: 0.50 to 16.30, P = 0.01); 
and other administration routes (SMD = 2.49, 95% CI: 0.02 to 4.97, P = 0.04).
Subsequently, we found that IL-4 levels were also significantly increased in the vaccine group (overall 
SMD = 2.98, 95% CI: 1.54 to 4.43, P < 0.0001), with moderate-to-high between-study heterogeneity (I 2 = 65%, 
P = 0.009). Subgroup analyses stratified by administration route showed significant effects: intramuscular 
injection (SMD = 2.29, 95% CI: 1.05 to 3.53, P = 0.0003); intranasal injection (SMD = 9.10, 95% CI: 6.57 to 24.76, 
P = 0.26); and other administration routes (SMD = 8.83, 95% CI: 3.71 to 13.96, P = 0.0007). The effect for the 
subcutaneous injection subgroup could not be estimated due to unavailability of data. Significant differences in 
effect size were detected among subgroups (P = 0.04, I2 = 69.5%), suggesting that administration route serves as 
an important source of between-study heterogeneity (Fig. 11).

Discussion
circRNA is a covalently closed circular RNA molecule that has garnered significant attention due to its exceptional 
durability and stability, offering promise as a stable platform for gene expression delivery 40,41. In this study, we 
first performed a systematic meta-analysis to investigate the therapeutic efficacy of lipid nanoparticle (LNP)-
delivered circRNA in mouse tumor models. Consistent and marked tumor regression was observed across 
all included mouse models, and notably, no significant alterations in body weight were detected throughout 
the treatment period. This finding not only confirms the potent antitumor activity of circRNA vaccines but 
also indicates a favorable safety profile, supporting LNP-circRNA as a safe and effective therapeutic strategy 
for tumor intervention. Accumulating evidence has established that the efficacy of therapeutic tumor vaccines

is predominantly dependent on efficient antigen presentation and the subsequent activation of dendritic cells 
(DCs)—the key professional antigen-presenting cells in the immune system 42. Consequently, we analyzed 
immune cell states to elucidate the mechanism by which circRNA vaccines exert their effects. Results revealed 
marked proliferation and activation of T cells, DCs, and other immune cells following vaccination, suggesting 
that circRNA exerts potent antitumor effects by modulating the innate immune system. These results collectively 
suggest that circRNA vaccines exert their potent antitumor effects by modulating the immune system to initiate a 
cascade of adaptive immune responses, ultimately eliminating tumor cells. Subsequently, we examined circRNA 
vaccine efficacy against infectious diseases. Findings demonstrated a significantly reduced viral load in mouse 
lungs post-vaccination, with sustained high antibody titers over extended periods The concurrent activation 
of innate and adaptive immune cells in infectious disease models further corroborated the broad and potent 
therapeutic efficacy of circRNA vaccines against both malignant tumors and infectious pathogens16.
To further validate the potent anti-tumor and immune-activating effects of circRNA vaccines, we conducted 
in vitro verification experiments. To prepare high-purity circRNA and exclude non-specific immune interference, 
we combined RNase R-mediated digestion (to eliminate linear RNA precursors and fragments) with RNase III 
treatment (to specifically degrade dsRNA impurities), followed by secondary purification using a general silica 
column. Results demonstrated that purified circRNA successfully induced neoantigen expression in dendritic 
cells (DCs), while promoting DC activation and maturation, consistent with the findings of Huang et al. 43. We 
further confirmed that circRNA-primed mature DCs could effectively present antigens to T cells and mediate 
the activation of antigen-specific T lymphocytes. These activated T cells subsequently secrete proinflammatory 
cytokines, including IL-1β, TNF-α, IL-6, and IFN-γ. ELISpot experiments validate that antigen-specific T cells 
can be generated, which in turn orchestrate a robust antitumor immune microenvironment and promote tumor 
cell killing, consistent with the results of Amaya et al.15.
Collectively, our systematic meta-analysis and in vitro experimental validation consistently demonstrate that 
circRNA vaccines possess the capacity to elicit robust and sustained innate and adaptive immune responses, as 
well as to shape a proinflammatory tumor microenvironment. Endowed with multiple inherent advantages—
including a covalently closed circular structure conferring superior stability, sustained and efficient neoantigen 
expression, and excellent in vivo tolerability—circRNA-encoded neoantigen vaccines represent a novel and 
promising therapeutic approach for the treatment of both malignant tumors and infectious diseases.

Notwithstanding these promising findings, several notable limitations and sources of substantial statistical 
heterogeneity must be acknowledged, which temper the interpretation and generalizability of our results. First, 
the meta-analysis revealed extreme statistical heterogeneity across the included studies for key immune and 
therapeutic outcome measures. Although we attempted to reduce heterogeneity through subgroup analysis, high 
heterogeneity persisted in some datasets. Comprehensive subgroup analysis indicated that this variability was 
primarily attributable to three interrelated factors: different routes of vaccine administration (e.g., intramuscular, 
subcutaneous, intravenous, intranasal), heterogeneity of disease models (e.g., different solid tumor types, viral 
pathogens), and diverse murine strains (C57BL/6, BALB/c, NOD/SCID) across the included studies. For instance, 
intranasal administration was associated with more pronounced local mucosal immune responses but relatively 
modest systemic immune effects, whereas intravenous injection elicited robust systemic immune activation.
Second, the current study is predominantly based on preclinical murine models, and the meta-analysis was 
restricted to animal experimental data due to the paucity of available clinical research on circRNA vaccines to 
date. There are inherent biological differences between murine models and humans, including disparities in 
immune system regulation (e.g., DC subsets, T cell receptor repertoires) and tumor microenvironment, which 
may result in inconsistent efficacy and safety profiles when translating circRNA vaccines from preclinical models 
to clinical applications. Although we supplemented our findings with in vitro validation using human PBMCs, 
this simplified system cannot fully recapitulate the complex in vivo microenvironment of the human body.
Finally, the detailed molecular mechanisms underlying the immune modulation of circRNA vaccines remain 
incompletely elucidated. While our results confirm that circRNA vaccines activate DCs and initiate adaptive 
immune responses, the specific molecular pathways by which LNP-circRNA is internalized by DCs and the 
potential crosstalk between circRNA and other innate immune sensors require further in-depth mechanistic 
investigation.
Despite these limitations, this study provides comprehensive and rigorous preclinical evidence for the 
therapeutic potential of LNP-delivered circRNA vaccines against both tumors and infectious diseases through 
systematic meta-analysis and in vitro validation with high-purity circRNA. Future research should focus 
on addressing the aforementioned limitations and heterogeneity to advance the development and clinical 
translation of circRNA vaccines. Priority areas include: conducting in-depth mechanistic studies to elucidate 
the molecular basis of circRNA uptake, cytoplasmic translation, and immune activation, thereby identifying 
potential molecular targets for enhancing vaccine efficacy; establishing more clinically relevant humanized 
mouse models to bridge the gap between preclinical and clinical research; and conducting large-scale, well-
designed randomized controlled trials to validate the efficacy and safety of circRNA vaccines in human subjects. 
In conclusion, our study highlights the great potential of circRNA vaccines as a novel next-generation RNA 
vaccine platform, and with further optimization and in-depth validation, circRNA vaccines are expected to 
provide innovative and effective therapeutic options for human diseases.
Conclusion
This article demonstrates that LNP-delivered circRNA vaccines exhibit potent anti-tumor and anti-inflammatory 
effects, with minimal adverse reactions. Further experiments validate the capacity of circRNA to encode 
neoantigens, promote dendritic cell maturation, activate T cells, and stimulate the secretion of inflammatory 
cytokines, positioning it as a promising novel therapeutic approach for cancer treatment.
