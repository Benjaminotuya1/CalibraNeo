Reviewer comments: 
Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This manuscript by Liu and colleagues reports a preclinical study into a circular RNA vaccine to prevent Zika virus (ZIKV)
infection. The authors compared 3 different constructs of circRNA to identify one that fused EDIII of ZIKV with a human IgG1
Fc fragment to form a EDIII dimer. They show that circRNA expressing EDIII-Fc produced superior immunogenicity
compared to the other 2 constructs in adult female mice, and this superior immunogenicity translated to better protection
against lethal ZIKV infection in pups from the vaccinated females. They also showed the the antibody response produced
from vaccination cross-reacted minimally with DENVs and did not enhance DENV infection to levels seen with antisera
against ZIKV. Finally, they showed that with additional modification, such as the use of IRES of iHRV-B3, the vaccine had
the potential of being a single dose application with even better protection compared to their original construct. They
concluded that their findings set a foundation for developing circRNA flavivirus vaccines. 
This is an interesting study although there are several concerns that need to be addressed. These are: 
1. Congenital Zika syndrome (CZS) is caused by ZIKV infection in expectant mothers, where transplacental transmission to
the developing fetus dysregulates normal CNS development. In this study, ZIKV is inoculated to pups from vaccinated dams.
Although I do not think the study design makes the findings less robust, a discussion on the differences in infection route
between the experimental approach and real-world infection, along with their implication on vaccine-mediated protection
against CZS should be clearly discussed. 
2. Although the authors argued for the merits of circRNA, the dose used in this study – 20 microgram – is high. Conventional
COVID-19 nucleoside-modified linear mRNA vaccines were showed efficacy against lethal SARS-CoV-2 infection in mice
at a dose of 5 micrograms. The higher dose would mean not only higher cost, it could also lead to more adverse events and
increase the risk of severe adverse events. Moreover, there is no comparison between circRNA and linear, nucleoside
modified mRNA that express the same 3 different EDIII constructs. Thus, while the authors argued for the merits of circRNA,
there is no data to show that circRNA is a better system to deliver the EDIII-Fc transgene. 
3. Lines 56-57. This statement is not true. There are Zika vaccine candidates that have completed phase I clinical trial with
useful safety outcomes. Their development has been stalled by the global low incidence of Zika cases. Please correct this
statement. 
4. Lines 58-59. Zika vaccine development has not been impeded by the concern of ADE of DENV infection. Please see
explanation in comment #3. 
5. Line 74. It is not helpful to mix the pathology of dengue with that of Zika. Coagulopathy and vascular leakage is more
pertinent to dengue than Zika. 
6. Line 122. Description of results in this line and elsewhere should be described as objectively as possible. Please avoid
superlatives, such as “greatly”. 
7. Lines 144-147 and Figure 1K. Although the differences in CD8+ T cell response to the different vaccine constructs raises
interesting questions on the expression or processing of the different EDIII constructs for MHC class I presentation, it raises
concern for EDIII-Fc as a human vaccine. Human CD8+ T cell epitopes are found mostly elsewhere in the ZIKV and
flaviviral proteome. If the protection seen in subsequent experiments, particularly those on adult IFNAR-/- mice were to be
mediated, even in parts, by CD8+ T cells, then the vaccine efficacy seen in this mouse model may over-estimate the efficacy
that can be expected in humans. Indeed, this possibility is raised by the data in Figures 4h and 5k, where the neutralising
antibody titers produced after vaccination were not at levels sufficient for sterilizing immunity, as evidenced by detectable
viremia even for the most optimal construct. Control of infection at later time points could thus have been mediated by
cellular immunity, given that we now know anamnestic T cell response is faster than B cell response (Painter et al, Nat
Immunol 2023; 24:1711-1724). 
8. Line 248 and 384-392. Please specify the strain of mouse-adapted DENV-2 used in this study. What was the parental
strain of this mouse-adapted DENV-2? Moreover, it is puzzling that the authors generated this strain by inoculating C57Bl/6
mice subcutaneously and could then harvest the virus from the brains of these animals. The reference they cited (Orozco et
al, J Gen Virol 2012) used AG129 mice and passaged the virus for 11 cycles and not just 3 passages. Is this correct? 
9. Lines 250-260. These statements misrepresent the findings in Figures 4d, 4e and 4g. Even sera from vaccinated animals
led mortality and viremia in this model. Moreover, p=0.9179 in the survival curve for EN(LNP) vaccinated mice vs uninfected
controls is simply unbelievable given that over 50% of the mice died from infection. 
10. Figure 4a and Lines 914-916. This set of data cannot be shown in such a way. The IgG titers were measured using
different approaches. Those for ZIKV and DENV1 used only EDIII whereas those against the other DENVs used whole E
proteins. The E proteins are larger and hence the amount of antigens on the coated plates would have fewer antigens than
those coated with EDIII. The data from the ELISAs are not comparable. Showing this set of data in such a way is misleading.
11. There are several grammatical and typographical errors that need to be corrected. 
Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This manuscript described the development of a circular RNA vaccine to prevent ZIKV infection with reduced risk of causing
ADE of subsequent DENV infection. The authors tested multiple circRNA constructs expressing Zika EDIII in monomer,
dimer, or trimer conformation and determined the EDIII-Fc dimer is the most superior confirmation. The addition of NS1 into
the vaccine formulation (EN) further improved maternal passive protection to neonatal mice from brain damage/inflammation
than vaccine with EDIII-Fc or NS1 circRNA alone. The EN circRNA molecules encapsulated in LNP separately (EN(LNP))
or together (EN(RNA)) resulted in similar immunogenicity and protection outcomes. However, 2 doses of maternal
immunization were required to provide complete protection from detectable ZIKV infection in neonatal mouse brains. Using
an adult INFAR-/- mouse model, they also demonstrated both EN formulations elicited rapid protection (12 days) against
illness and reduced viremic RNA post ZIKV infection. 
To further optimize the EN(LNP) vaccine candidate, the PIE elements of the plasmid were modified. The optimization
improved translation of EDIII, NS1, and Gluc in transfected cells and the optimized (opt) EN(LNP) provided better
immunogenicity and protection than the prototype EN(LNP). A single high (20-ug) dose of the opt-EN(LNP) provided
complete protection in both passive and active immunization mouse models. A longer protection duration was observed in
neonatal mice born 11 weeks after maternal immunization with a high dose of the opt vaccine. 
Most of the study results
supported the conclusion that a single dose of the opt-EN(LNP) ZIKV vaccine afforded high protection efficacy against ZIKV
in the 2 tested mouse models. The manuscript detailed the steps from selecting an optimal EDIII conformation, rationale of
including NS1, and optimization of the vaccine to be effective with a single dose. Overall, the study is very interesting and
provides new knowledge in use of the circRNA platform for ZIKV vaccine development. However, there is a major concern
related to the ADE evaluation of the vaccine and the strong conclusions regarding the vaccine ADE potential in the
manuscript. 
Based on the rationale of the viral antigens used for the vaccine, it is reasonable to speculate that the vaccine would induce
less ADE-prone cross-reactive Abs to DENVs than other vaccine using prM-E or whole E. However, the in vitro and in vivo
ADE evaluation conducted in the study had some significant flaws and limitations to really support their strong conclusion
that the vaccine would be ADE-free. The title, abstract, and many areas of the manuscript all emphasized that the vaccine
has proven to be: “without enhancing dengue severity”, “without DENV ADE”, and “ADE-free”, but I have questions
regarding the ADE experimental data used to support such conclusion. 
List of the specific concerns/comments: 
1. The manuscript writing is fairly clear and understandable but seems somewhat choppy in some areas. Some minor
editorial modification can be useful to make the manuscript more concise with smoother flow and connection, while
providing more clarification in some areas to improve the reading experience. 
2. In vitro ADE evaluation (Fig 4b and 6g-i): should the y-axis be expressed in percent (%)? Below are questions in the data
presented in the graphs. 
a. The “relative infectivity” normalized to “peak of ZIKV infection” in the Fig 4 and 6 did not make sense for presenting the
DENV ADE curves. The relative infectivity should be calculated against infectivity of each virus without Ab treatment (base
line infectivity for each specific virus in the graph). E.g., DENV-1 infectivity (without sera) would be the 100% for the DENV-1
graph. In our experience, ZIKV typically replicates much faster than DENVs in mammalian cells, so using the peak ADE
ZIKV infectivity by diluted Zika sera to normalize the graph for all viruses would result in very low DENV infectivity curves as
shown in 4b. Due to its slower replication kinetics than ZIKV, there would be less DENV-positive cells than the ZIKV-
positive cells detected by flow cytometry from samples collected at the same time point (2 days pi). Also, Line 636 indicated
the moi used for ZIKV and DENV-3 was 5-fold higher (at 1) than other DENVs (at 0.2). The higher moi used for ZIKV
infection would further skew the results for a much higher ZIKV infectivity than those DENVs infected at lower moi. The
skewed graph scale made the interpretation of the DENV ADE curves difficult. 
b. Even with the flawed ADE curve presentation, these graphs suggested that there were DENV ADE effects at the lowest
dilutions of the vaccine sera. The full DENV ADE curves were not captured from the tested vaccination sera dilution range,
and there is no standard Ab quantity (e.g. protein mass) to be used for X-axis. Therefore, it is difficult for an accurate ADE
potential assessment or comparison from the graphs. 
On a good note, Fig 4a demonstrated that the IgG elicited by vaccine candidates had significantly less proportion of DENV
cross-reactive Ab than the ZIKV control (using anti-Zika IgG as the total IgG). Therefore, Fig 4a could support the notion that
the vaccine candidates elicited significantly less DENV ADE-prone Abs than the live ZIKV infection. Unfortunately, similar
anti-ZIKV and anti-DENV IgG were not measured for the final optimized EN(LNP). Would suggest to also conduct this
analysis for the opt vaccine in Fig 6. 
3. Fig 4 c-h: The in vivo ADE evaluation of the prototype vaccine candidates. The data was used to suggest passive transfer
of vaccination sera or Ab elicited by vaccination did not enhance the severity of illness or enhance viremic RNA after DENV-
2 challenge. In my opinion, the results could only suggest the single dose of the Ab in the 20ul sera (Fig.4c) or the Ab on 14
days after immunization of the vaccine (Fig 4f) did not cause ADE illness by DENV-2 challenge. Since the Ab concentration
is an important factor for ADE, the single passive-transferred sera dose (4c) or the single DENV2 challenge time point (4f)
may not be suitable for ADE evaluation of the vaccines. It might be better to use another non-replicative Zika vaccine with
ADE-prone antigens (e.g. inactivated ZIKV, recombinant E, or VLP) as a positive ADE control for the ADE study. 
The blood Ab titers to ZIKV and DENV2 at time (2wks) of challenge might enable better interpretation, but they were not
measured in the study. However, based on the Fig 2g study, only 1 animal has measurable ZIKV nAb 2wks after 1st
immunization of EDIII-Fc which suggests the 2wks time point for the DENV2 ADE evaluation might be too early (e.g. Ab
levels were below the required threshold for ADE at the time of DENV2 infection). Interestingly, the in vivo ADE evaluation
was not conducted for the final optimized vaccine candidate. Therefore, there is no animal ADE data to support the
conclusion that the single-dose opt-EN(LNP) would not cause severe DENV illness as suggested in the manuscript title.
Mouse models are not very good for DENV or flavivirus ADE evaluation in general. Many flaviviruses without any evidence
of causing ADE in humans would cause ADE in mouse models. Many biological components involved in ADE are very
different between mouse and human, so caution should be exercised in using mice or in vitro ADE outcomes for human
vaccine candidates. Discussions in the limitations of the ADE models should at least be included. Suggest removing or
significantly toning down statements, such as “without DENV ADE”, “ADE-free”, and other similar claims throughout the
manuscript. 
4. The title: Suggest removing “without enhancement of dengue severity” and add “in mice” at the end or “prevents mice
from” Zika virus infection. 
5. Line 94: Need more background and citation of the IgG1 FC fusion to form EDIII dimer. Would there be any other
immunogenic effect from the fusion Fc? 
6. Line 104-110: please provide more background/introduction regarding the T4 ferritin for EDIII trimer formation, PIE
method, and the reasons for the specific PIE element choice in the prototype plasmid Ana (e.g., why iCVB3 IRES was
chosen, what are the spacer sequences used in this plasmid). 
7. Line 118: Suggest adding a brief summary of the encapsulation efficiency and sizes of the circRNA-LNP, as they were
mentioned in the Methods (line 483-486). 
8. Lines 150 and 166: Suggest specifying what protective effects are being tested at the beginning of each section, such as
protection of neonatal mice through passive maternal immunity (line 150) versus protection adult mice by active
immunization (line 166). 
9. Line 153, 553, and Fig 2a: Line 153 states mice were mated 3 weeks after immunization but indicated 2 weeks in Fig 2a
and line 553. 
10. Line 263-269 and 339-342: More background and discussion of the optimized PIE elements in the construct should be
provided. What is the rationale to change to these element sequences (e.g., reason of change the IRES to iHV-B3 with Apt-
elF4G, change of spacer UTRs, etc.)? 
11. Line 273: Why was the EN(RNA) vaccine taken out of contention against the EN(LNP) vaccine at this point? Both
vaccines provided similar immunogenicity and protection, so it would be helpful to provide the reason of the EN(LNP)
selection for further optimization development. Is the selection based on certain advantage (e.g., higher LNP encapsulation
rate, easier vaccine formulation) or just by chance? 
12. Line 562: should define FNT. 
13. Line 588: change “teasing” to “passing”? 
14. Line 865, Fig 2a: Line 865 states IFNAR -/- mice were challenged 2 weeks after 2nd immunization. Figure 2a says they
were challenged 3 weeks after immunization. 
15. Figure 3A: The mating schedule is a little unclear from this image. Suggest changing the branch out point immediately
after 1st immunization (before 2wks). Based on where the two lines branch out at the current scheme, it looks like two rounds
of mating occurred in the 2-dose study line. 
16. Sup Fig 1a and 9a: what is the meaning of “optional” indicated in both plasmid figures? 
17. Sup Fig 1h and 9e: It would be useful to provide a purpose of the junction site PCR and sequence validation. 
18. nAb was measured after immunization, but was never measured after virus challenging from the survivors. The post
challenge nAb titers would be useful to determine whether the vaccine provided a solid sterile immunity (no boost effect of
the post challenge nAb titer). It is somewhat surprising that it was not done for the vaccine study.

Nature manuscript # 492562_0 
In the manuscript entitled “ A single dose circular RNA vaccine completely prevents Zika virus infection without enhancing
dengue severity” by Liu et al., the authors developed ZIKV vaccines in a form of circular RNA encoding the envelop protein
domain III 
and NS1 and reported the efficacy of the vaccine candidates using C57BL/6 and IFNAR-/- mouse strains. 
The
study results showed that maternal immunity elicited by circRNA encoding dimeric EDIII, fused to human IgG1 Fc fragment
(EDIII-Fc) were superior to circRNA encoding monomeric or trimeric EDII in terms of 
activation of germinal center reactions,
facilitating high neutralizing antibody production and induction of CD4/CD8 T cells producing cytokines. 
One-day-old
neonates born from dams immunized with either one- or two dose vaccines survived after lethal challenge but do not prevent
viral infection and morbidity. CircRNA encoding either ZIKV EDIII-Fc or NS1 alone provides partial protection against ZIKV
infection and developmental retardation. Further, optimized circRNA EN(LNP), which increases stability and protein
translation, reduced viral RNA burden in the neonatal brains and increase protective efficacy 
that was durable up to 12
weeks. The authors concluded that 
two-dose immunizations of circRNA EN (LNP) provide full protection against ZIKV
infection while avoiding ADE of DENV, but not by a single dose immunization. The results highlight that circRNA could be a
platform for a safe and effective flavivirus vaccine. 
Major comments: 
Overall, the manuscript is well-written. The studies were well- designed with proper controls. The authors made thorough
efforts to improve the efficacy of LNP encapsulated optimized circRNA vaccine encoding EDIII and NS1. The study results
showed promising outcomes of protection of adult and neonate C57BL/6 and IFANR-/- mice against ZIKV infection while
avoiding possible ADE of DENV, which is a significant risk factor of flavivirus infection. However, I have a few concerns
regarding data presentation, and the conclusion in the discussion was overstated and not fully supported by the data
presented in the current version of manuscript. I suggest that the authors revise the conclusion to adhere to the results. 
See
below the specific comment below.

Reviewer #1 (Remarks to the Author): 
This manuscript by Liu and colleagues reports a preclinical study into a circular RNA 
vaccine to prevent Zika virus (ZIKV) infection. The authors compared 3 different 
constructs of circRNA to identify one that fused EDIII of ZIKV with a human IgG1 Fc 
fragment to form a n EDIII dimer. They show that circRNA expressing EDIII -Fc 
produced superior immunogenicity compared to the other 2 constructs in adul t female 
mice, and this superior immunogenicity translated to better protection against lethal 
ZIKV infection in pups from the vaccinated females. They also showed th at the 
antibody response produced from vaccination cross -reacted minimally with DENVs 
and did not enhance DENV infection to levels seen with antisera against ZIKV . Finally, 
they showed that with additional modification, such as the use of IRES of iHRV -B3, 
the vaccine had the potential of being a single dose application with even better 
protection compared to their original construct. They concluded that their findings set 
a foundation for developing circRNA flavivirus vaccines. 
This is an interesting study although there are several concerns that need to be addressed.  
These are: 
1. Congenital Zika syndrome (CZS) is caused by ZIKV infection in expectant mothers, 
where transplacental transmission to the developing fetus dysregulates normal CNS 
development. In this study, ZIKV is inoculated to pups from vaccinated dams. Although 
I do not think the study design makes the findings less robust, a discussion on the 
differences in infection route between the experimental approach and real -world 
infection, along with their implication on vaccine -mediated protection against CZS 
should be clearly discussed. 
Response: We agree with the notion that ZIKV infection in pregnant animals, such 
as immunocompetent mice and marmosets, are more relevant to  the CZS observed in 
real world (Kim IJ et al., NPJ Vaccines 2022; Kim IJ et al., NPJ Vaccines 2024). Though 
our study's model of maternal immunization and neonatal challenge doesn't mimic 
human CZS as closely as pregnancy models in other studies (Kim IJ et al., NPJ Vaccines 
2022; Kim IJ et al., NPJ Vaccines 2024 ), it may represent a stringent model for 
evaluating the protective efficacy of maternal immunity. Unlike the pregnan t mice 
models wherein both nAbs and T cell responses contribute to restricting the inoculated 
ZIKV from crossing the placental barriers ( Kim IJ et al., NPJ Vaccines 2024; Ngono 
AE et al., Sci Adv 2020), in the challenged pups only the nAbs inherited from the dams 
can confer protection. In addition, it has been reported that in pregnant mice, the 
monocytes or macrophages in the placenta play significant roles in inhibiting ZIKV 
transmission to the fetus, another barrier absent in the challenged pups (Winkler CW et 
al., J Immunol 2020). In this respect, our results support that EN(LNP), especially the 
optimized version, gives rise to protective immun ity of high magnitude and quality, 
thereby conferring complete protection against ZIKV infection in this stringent model  
(Revised Fig. 6 and Fig. 7 ). We have added this discussion to the revised manuscript 
(lines 514-526), following your insightful suggestion. 
 
2. Although the authors argued for the merits of circRNA, the dose used in this study – 
20 microgram – is high. Conventional COVID -19 nucleoside-modified linear mRNA 
vaccines were showed efficacy against lethal SARS-CoV-2 infection in mice at a dose 
of 5 micrograms. The higher dose woul d mean not only higher cost, it could also lead 
to more adverse events and increase the risk of severe adverse events. Moreover, there 
is no comparison between circRNA and linear, nucleoside modified mRNA that express 
the same 3 different EDIII constructs. Thus, while the authors argued for the merits of 
circRNA, there is no data to show that circRNA is a better system to deliver the EDIII-
